\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

Bug reports provide developers with information about problems encountered during software use. Their usefulness depends on whether the reported behavior and the circumstances in which it occurred can be understood. Zimmermann et al. identified a mismatch between the information developers consider useful and the information reporters can provide: reproduction steps, stack traces, and test cases were valued by developers but could be difficult for reporters to supply \cite{zimmermann2010}. Soltani et al. subsequently examined report elements through interviews, a developer survey, and repository analysis, reinforcing the importance of reproduction steps and contextual information \cite{soltani2020}.

Missing information is not limited to diagnostic artifacts. Chaparro et al. found that expected behavior and steps to reproduce were often absent from bug descriptions \cite{chaparro2017}. In a study of web application reports, Wang et al. found that usage information was frequently added after the initial submission \cite{wang2025}. These findings motivate assistance that helps reporters describe their observations and relevant circumstances before submitting a report. They do not imply that every report needs the same technical details.

Interactive assistance is an established direction in bug reporting. BURT guides end-users through reporting observed behavior, expected behavior, and reproduction steps, using immediate feedback and application-specific information \cite{song2022}. More recent approaches investigate LLM-based report improvement and external context: ChatBR assesses and improves report elements, while ImproBR combines report improvement with retrieved domain knowledge \cite{chatbr2024,akyol2026}. The present thesis builds on these directions by investigating documentation-informed clarification in an interaction with the reporter.

The proposed system will use a large language model (LLM) to identify omissions or ambiguity and formulate follow-up questions. Documentation accessed through the Model Context Protocol (MCP) will provide information about the service's capabilities and previously resolved problems. MCP defines mechanisms for exposing resources and callable tools; deciding which documents are relevant and how to use them remains a responsibility of the reporting system \cite{mcp2025resources,mcp2025tools}.

The motivation is to examine whether this combination helps service users provide more complete and understandable reports while preserving their observations. This requires assessing both the resulting information and the effort involved in supplying it. A longer or more fluent report is not sufficient evidence of success: generated details can be unsupported by the source information, as discussed in research on hallucination in natural-language generation \cite{ji2023}.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The problem addressed in this thesis is the collection of sufficiently complete, clear, and accurate information from users reporting problems with a company's software services. A report may describe a symptom without specifying the actions that preceded it, the expected outcome, or relevant preconditions. Existing research establishes the occurrence of such omissions and the importance of additional context \cite{chaparro2017,wang2025}. The research problem is to determine whether an intelligent reporting interaction can address these omissions effectively in the selected support setting.

The intended organizational clients are companies that can integrate the reporting system into support processes for their own software services. The system is conceived as a component that can be adapted to a company's services and documentation, without requiring a particular software delivery model. The project is an independently developed research prototype with no industrial partner. Its evaluation will use controlled support scenarios rather than assume an existing industrial deployment.

The organizations adopting the system and the people submitting reports have different roles. Reporters are users of the adopting company's services, while developers and support specialists receive the structured reports. The reporting interaction should accommodate users with different levels of technical knowledge. It does not require them to identify the cause of a problem or have access to source code and internal diagnostic tools.

The intended intake covers user-observed problems such as an unresponsive button, an incorrectly displayed interface, an error during an operation, or uncertainty about how to use a service. The last category includes cases in which a user does not know that a prerequisite or an earlier action is required. These are examples of the supported reporting context, not an exhaustive list of defects or a claim that all problem types have been evaluated. An incoming support request is not necessarily a confirmed software bug: it may describe a defect, unexpected behavior, or a misunderstanding of the documented workflow. The system's task is to clarify and record the problem so that the technical team has an adequate description for further assessment.

For each adopting company, the planned documentation collection will describe its service's capabilities and previously resolved problems. The system will access this collection through MCP and use relevant material to ask questions about the operation attempted, its inputs, and applicable conditions. For example, a documented prerequisite may motivate a question about whether the reporter completed the required action. Documentation about another incident can suggest what to clarify, but it does not establish what happened in the current case. The final report will organize information supplied or confirmed by the reporter and preserve unresolved details as such.

The interface is intended to accept users' descriptions and provide clarification questions in their chosen language. It will then produce a structured report for developers. The languages evaluated and the specific service workflows and documentation used will be defined in the design and evaluation chapters. This requirement provides a direction for implementation; it does not establish equivalent reporting quality across all languages.

The central uncertainty is whether the proposed system, combining adaptive questions with service documentation accessed through MCP, improves reporting outcomes relative to a conventional structured form. Additional questions may collect useful information while also increasing reporting time or workload. The thesis therefore treats usefulness as an empirical question involving report quality, factual fidelity, and user effort. The comparison evaluates the complete system rather than the individual contribution of its components.

\section{Aim and Objectives}
\label{sec:aim}

The aim of this thesis is to develop an intelligent system that companies can integrate into their software support processes and evaluate how LLM-assisted clarification, informed by service documentation accessed through MCP, affects report quality and reporting effort in controlled support scenarios.

The following objectives support this aim:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Review research on bug report quality, interactive reporting, LLM-based report improvement, and the use of external context.
    \item Define representative reporting scenarios, relevant report elements, and a documentation collection covering service capabilities and previously resolved problems.
    \item Design and implement a reporting prototype that detects missing or ambiguous information, asks targeted clarification questions, and structures the reporter's confirmed answers.
    \item Integrate access to service documentation through MCP and use retrieved information to inform clarification questions.
    \item Prepare a conventional structured form as a baseline for comparison with the complete intelligent reporting system.
    \item Evaluate report completeness, clarity, and fidelity to user-provided information, together with reporting time, interaction effort, and perceived workload.
    \item Analyze the results to identify the benefits and limitations of the system as a whole.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

The objectives concern building and studying the system. The following research questions specify the outcomes that the evaluation will investigate:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*:},leftmargin=*]
    \item \label{rq:quality} How does an LLM-assisted reporting form affect the completeness and clarity of bug reports compared with a conventional structured form?
    \item \label{rq:effort} How does the form affect reporting time, interaction effort, and perceived workload?
    \item \label{rq:fidelity} How faithfully do the final reports preserve user-confirmed information, and what unsupported details, if any, are introduced?
\end{enumerate}

These three questions allow improvements, trade-offs, or a lack of measurable benefit. RQ1 and RQ2 address report quality and reporting effort, respectively. RQ3 examines whether the output remains faithful to the reporter's evidence. In all three questions, the assisted form denotes the complete system with documentation access through MCP. Chapter~\ref{chap:lr} explains how the reviewed literature motivates these questions; the evaluation chapter will define their measurements and analysis.

\section{Scope and Expected Contribution}
\label{sec:scope}

The scope covers the preparation of reports about user-observed software problems, from an initial description through clarification to a structured report for technical assessment. The intake includes apparent functional failures, interface problems, execution errors, and difficulties understanding how to use a service. Reports about suspected defects are the focus of the bug reporting research questions; requests arising from unclear usage or prerequisites provide a related support context and will be distinguished in the evaluation. Collecting such requests does not imply that the system will automatically determine whether a software defect exists.

The prototype will combine a user-facing reporting interface, LLM-assisted information collection, and documentation access through MCP. Integration is intended to connect the reporting process to the adopting company's documentation. The service documentation and resolved cases used in the study will be identified so that their provenance and relevance can be assessed. Adaptability to different companies is a design objective; the evaluation will establish results only for the workflows and materials actually studied.

The study focuses on what the reporting interaction collects and communicates. Report completeness concerns relevant information rather than the completion of every possible field. Clarity concerns whether the description is understandable, while fidelity concerns whether observations and confirmed answers are preserved without unsupported additions. The evaluation will consider these properties alongside the effort required from the reporter and the relevance of the system's questions.

Root-cause identification, automated code repair, and deployment of fixes are outside the scope. A more complete problem description may support later investigation, but improvements in reporting will not be presented as evidence of faster diagnosis or resolution. Such claims would require measurements of those downstream activities. Research on non-reproducible reports also shows that reproduction difficulties can arise from environmental differences and dependencies between bugs, beyond the wording of a report \cite{joorabchi2014}.

The expected contribution is a working prototype and a comparative evaluation of documentation-informed reporting assistance. The contribution lies in implementing and assessing the complete reporting interaction in the defined setting, including its factual fidelity and the effort required from users. The comparison will support conclusions about the system as a whole, without attributing observed differences specifically to documentation access or another individual component. The conclusions will be bounded by the service scenario, reporting tasks, languages, documentation, and participants included in the study.

\section{Thesis Structure}
\label{sec:structure}

The thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~2 reviews bug report quality, structured and interactive reporting, LLM-assisted report improvement, documentation access through MCP, and evaluation practices. It synthesizes the literature in relation to the research questions introduced in this chapter. Chapter~3 will describe the service scenario, requirements, documentation sources, and system architecture. Chapter~4 will present the implementation, including the reporting interaction, documentation integration, and generation of structured reports. Chapter~5 will specify the evaluation methodology and report and discuss the results, including limitations and threats to validity. Chapter~6 will summarize the findings and outline directions for further work.
